# Introduction

One thing I learned as a minor enthusiast of the arts—and, among them,
cinema—is that retelling a classic is a task for when you have baggage.
If you are a beginner, you probably have nothing relevant to offer a
classic work. That is not an insult; it is a fact. Works become classics because
they condense an enormous amount of timeless truths and themes that
touch people's souls deeply when they are retold or staged. Whatever the
medium, the content of the work speaks directly to the soul.

Since that task is difficult, the first path is to create something that is very
specific, yet original enough to be an interesting story.
That is what we see when Christopher Nolan directed Memento and
Inception, spectacular stories that communicate
timeless subjects: how to be sure of a fact, how to know whether I can trust a
person, whether I think for myself.

Those works and many others demonstrated Nolan's ability to speak about
specific themes of the human condition in a very mature way. His Batman trilogy is nothing
more than a way of debating the dilemmas involving justice and hope.
And this great filmmaker decides to tell his version of the Odyssey, one of the
foundational texts of the Western canon. Obviously that is a strong claim; so
we can be sure that, at the very least, the Odyssey is a work that heavily influences
our Western culture. In the end, after so many acclaimed works, he
can afford to tell a classic story. He has already shown that he can
contribute something original even when revisiting a classic.

# About the Odyssey

The Odyssey is a very ancient epic poem attributed to Homer. For ancient
Greek civilization, it functioned almost like the Bible: interpreting the events of the
Iliad and the Odyssey was, for a Greek, the equivalent of what reading the Old Testament would be today.
That means it is a mythological story; mythology is not fantasy.
The intention of mythology is to speak about events so ancient that their symbolic weight
is stronger than the exact memory of when they occurred.

Many skeptics and relativists like to offer a somewhat allegorical or
fantastical interpretation of mythic texts. What matters is that the person who belongs to that
culture reads it immersed in that culture and has absorbed all the values that people
received by producing and consuming that text. That means the Odyssey is a
text that communicates directly with the human soul, with all its longings and its
tragedies.

Finally, we must take the Odyssey seriously as a text that has something important
to teach us about life: Odysseus's desire to return home, Telemachus's anguish
in the face of the absence of the father he never knew, or Penelope's fidelity in
believing in her beloved's return after so many years away from home. The Odyssey,
like every great foundational poem, teaches the ancient Greeks how they should deal
with their gods. For us Western Christians, it
contains ancient wisdom that has survived over time and gives us direction in this
modern chaos.

# About the adaptation

There was much speculation about how the work would be received given its casting.
The choices of Lupita Nyong'o as Helen of Troy and Elliot Page caused many expectations among
those who labeled the film as progressive pandering. I confess I saw no
problem with those casting choices, and the character I found most interesting,
and whose casting was very good, is Eurylochus, played by Himesh Patel. I really
bought that second-in-command's pain at having been loyal to Odysseus and, after years of
loyalty and suffering, being consumed by doubt and exhaustion—especially when hunger
pushes the crew toward decisions that seal his fate. You really buy his fidelity and you buy his fall
through lack of faith in the gods and in Odysseus's words. He was truly one of the characters
that marked me most.

Tom Holland as Telemachus is definitive. Of all the actors, he is the only one in whom I
did not see the famous actor, only the character. Tom is
a good actor, but I did not see a Greek Peter Parker trying to do the right thing
in a world full of drunkards harassing his mother. I truly saw a son who
deeply misses the father he never knew, resents his mother's situation, and
wants to assert himself as a man in the face of that whole situation, understanding himself as
his father's legitimate heir. I liked him immensely.

And it is indescribable how Eumaeus (John Leguizamo) is impactful. You see a
degree of masculinity in a simple man who is the most honorable figure in the entire work.
Robert Pattinson played the most despicable cowardly schemer in the film—and that is a
compliment. Eumaeus is a moral center of the work, and it is undeniable that John Leguizamo's performance
made all the difference. You buy his blindness, his limitations, his relationship with
Telemachus, with Penelope, with Argos, with the servants, and with Odysseus himself. I
would truly give an Oscar to that actor for this performance.

Everyone else, for me, stayed within what would be reasonable for
the work. Matt Damon convinces as Odysseus. Anne Hathaway as Penelope also
delivers a great performance, within her usual quality. Perhaps I did not expect
as much from Telemachus, Eumaeus, and Eurylochus; they surprised me. The others stayed within
my expectations regarding their professional competence. Perhaps the only one who,
incredibly, felt very ordinary is Jon Bernthal as Menelaus, whom I unfortunately
could not stop seeing as the Punisher dressed as a Greek king. Despite the characterization,
I still imagine Menelaus with a skull on his chest. But that is a detail;
in the end, the casting sold me on the Odyssey and its conflicts.

# About the mythology

I got a lot of spoilers that the cyclops's scream would be impactful, and it was! And that the
film's visual construction did not adopt a dull realism, but that did not mean
an embrace of an aesthetic bordering on fantasy. The film itself is quite sober in what
it chooses to imagine of the settings and constructions. It even seems that the money spent on
practical effects did not allow the scenes to be more colorful—which makes sense, since a
very colorful film with vivid colors does not suit Nolan. Considering
the message and the director's vision, the film is visually quite consistent.

The cyclops is a very strange and hard-to-stomach figure to look at. Yet it is a
genuinely interesting creation because he is not an imposing figure. He is visually
a cruel monster, capable of eating men without any problem. People said
I would buy the cyclops's side when he was blinded; I found the scene's pacing
too fast to manage that.
Since I already knew the outcome of that situation, I felt there was urgency and,
in the end, a feeling that "it already passed." What I mean is that the
scene's dynamics made it very clear that they would escape the cyclops's cave.

Still, all that urgency and speed is compensated by the cyclops's cry of pain.
What was created for that moment truly delivers everything it needed to.
You feel an agony mixed with a kind of indignation; it is truly inexplicable.
Nolan is always good at creating these scenes with a very impactful element that
gives you a unique experience.

The island of giants (Laestrygonians) is, in fact, the situation most successfully handled in the sense that you
have doubts about whether they would get out of there alive. The giants represented
all the dangers that the cyclops scene was unable to convey. It was a very interesting scene
from that point of view. The combination of armor with forced perspective—very tall stuntmen
opposed to smaller doubles—justifies the choice not to adhere to a more faithful historical
representation.

And speaking of faithful historical representation, it is really ugly to see that
Viking ship knowing that a trireme could have been there instead. It gives a very somber vibe—and,
I agree with Thiago Braga: a ship closer to the Greek representation would have been better. In Circe and Calypso, Nolan's
realistic approach became very clear. He could not characterize either of them in a
fantastical way because that goes against his nature. I neither liked nor disliked it; I just
thought: that is Nolan.

Finally, the other representations of mythological moments are what one would expect
from Nolan delivering his cinematic vision. I did not dislike it; I appreciated it within
the context I expect from a Nolan film.

# Conclusion

In the end, Nolan plants that little seed of doubt about whether what happened was real or
not and whether Athena was truly on his side the whole time. If you have not
watched Inception yet, you may not have understood the reference in that moment. I saw no
problem with that, but I ended up with the feeling that, if he was going to take that path,
it would have been interesting to bet on more fantastical mythological events. But then
I started wondering whether that would be viable. As the film goes on, you realize
there is a distinction between the mythic world of Odysseus's journey and the real world of
Ithaca. Ithaca is a concrete world, in which the dangers are men, where politics is
a violent and cruel power game. The journey, by contrast, works almost like a spiritual ordeal.

I do not know whether a more fantastical journey could have been balanced with an Ithaca
in decline. Indeed, a more fantastical and splendid aesthetic on the mythic side
could easily turn into farce upon arriving in the real world of Ithaca. I think
I am wanting the colorful film Nolan will never be able to make. And, in the
end, good mythology teaches you something even when you doubt what it has to tell
you.
