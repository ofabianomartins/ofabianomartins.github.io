# Introduction

This article is a brief reflection on the impact of AI on the day-to-day work of
software development. It is not meant to defend jobs or status for technology
professionals, but to clarify what product delivery should look like when the
tool changes. The thesis is simple: the real problem is not AI itself, but how
each developer reacts to it — and that reaction says more about ego and
self-awareness than about the technology.

# What AI Really Is and How It Should Be Viewed

My father is an industrial mechanics technician. His role has always been to
show up when things go wrong and to point out what to do so they do not go
wrong again. When an incident happened — that is, a production machine at the
company broke down — he would identify the problem and fix it. That might mean
just calibrating something, applying some product, or replacing a part. Replacing
a part could be done with a piece from a supplier or with a substitute part he
fabricated himself.

In many of those cases, he used a machine called a lathe: a shaft connected to
a motor, capable of spinning at high speed as needed. That rotation lets you
shape the part with symmetry around the axis — something much harder to do by
hand. As technology advanced, automatic lathes appeared: you parameterize the
part and the machine executes what was planned. I never saw my father wonder
whether the part had been made on the lathe or by hand. What mattered was
whether the part solved the problem.

Obviously, the leap between an automatic lathe and an LLM is enormous in
complexity and scope. But the principle is the same: what matters is treating
the tool as a tool. Many people still do not see LLMs that way. The code they
generate remains the responsibility of whoever instructed them — just as a lathe
part remains the responsibility of whoever designed the project. In the end,
what counts is how the result is applied.

Industry has been removing the human factor from the production chain as much
as possible — not out of ideology, but because it tends to be more efficient.
People take vacations, get sick, and need a long time to reach a uniform
standard of execution. Mechanization guarantees scale and cost reduction over
the long term; the final product gets cheaper and commoditization can follow.
This is nothing new: it is the natural course of technological evolution.

LLMs — commonly called AI — are one more step in that process. Many people fear
that AI will degrade software quality. That would be like imagining industrial
mechanics rejecting automatic lathes because they supposedly made worse parts.
That did not happen: a poorly specified part breaks and fails to solve the
problem, regardless of who or what made it. What defines value is the result,
not the instrument. No industrial technician prefers to make by hand a part that
the lathe delivers with greater precision. LLMs occupy, in software engineering,
the place that the lathe occupies in mechanics.

# The Psychology and Ego of the Programmer

Movies created two distorted views of programmers: 1) the socially incapable nerd
who took refuge in code; and 2) the genius capable of breaking cryptography and
getting the voluptuous woman at the end. Both are wrong. Programmers are ordinary
people — like physicists, chemists, and engineers — who, in general, develop
patience with a subject of study and train until they reach proficiency.

What sets the programmer apart is the ability to produce a lot of output without
interacting with another person. That does not mean interaction gets in the way;
only that the work can be done alone. Often that means hours of dedication in
which the immediate reward is the dopamine hit of reaching a result. Many
programmers have good social skills, but the problem-solving cycle weighs more:
when you solve on your own something the team could not, you get dopamine and
vanity together.

There is a lot of vanity in the profession. Part of what looks like secret
knowledge among programmers is, in fact, familiarity with something other people
do not have the patience to learn — and because it is everywhere in daily life,
it seems like magic. Nobody thinks a mechanic is magical: the trade requires a
specific environment and tools, and visible physical effort legitimizes the
result. For the programmer, the effort is mental and invisible; that is why the
work seems magical and the professional feels the need to defend it.

Paradoxically, many developers also enjoy that aura — it is the social reward
for the time invested in learning the craft. That dynamic — solving what nobody
around you can solve and harvesting others' dependence on a result that does not
seem to require effort — explains part of the profession's behavior.

With that in mind, developers can be grouped into four profiles, crossing
technical competence with the need for that social reward:

1. competent people who do not need it;
2. competent people who still need it;
3. less competent people who do not need it;
4. less competent people who need it.

The difference between groups 1 and 2 is not technical, but emotional. A member
of group 2 often carries impostor syndrome: they are functional, but have not
matured enough to stand on their own without external validation. Even so,
groups 1 and 2 tend to remain functional in the face of market changes. Group 3
has instrumentalized the profession in contexts where they deliver value perceived
as technological, without necessarily delivering technical depth. Group 4 has not
yet reached professional maturity and tends to externalize frustration — blaming
the market, the tool, or social dynamics instead of looking at their own gap.

There is no shame in recognizing which group you are in. The problem is not
knowing.

# What AI Has to Do with All of This

The way you react to AI indicates which group you are in and, by extension,
which path you need to follow.

**Group 1** sees AI as an automatic lathe: the result is worth what it solves,
not who — or what — produced it. They adopt the tool and gain productivity.

**Group 2** also gains a tool, but feels diminished for having "only instructed
the AI." They fear being replaceable by someone with less technical training who
learns to use the same instrument. It is not impossible — intelligent, dedicated
people can learn anything — but finding people like that, with time available,
is rarer than it seems.

**Group 3** uses AI to preserve the status quo: the tool does not fix a lack of
technical depth, it only masks it for a while.

**Group 4** splits into two distinct reactions:

- **4.1** — "AI is making my work seem invisible" (the same emotional wound as
  group 2, with less technical foundation);
- **4.2** — "I no longer need to study; AI solves everything" (the illusion of
  a shortcut).

Groups 1 and 3 adapt without an existential crisis — each in their own way,
with competence or with functional mediocrity. Those who need emotional work
are groups 2 and 4.1: learning to separate identity from instrument. I say this
without judgment — I have felt, and sometimes still feel, threatened by AI. I
notice that fear usually speaks more to ego than to real replacement.

**4.2** is a different case. Here the threat is concrete: those who believe AI
dispenses with study tend to fall behind, because the tool does not do the work
for you — it demands that you know even better what you are asking for and
validating.

# Conclusion

Your reaction to AI reveals more about you than about the technology. Even if
today it still does not deliver ideal cost-benefit for every case, the trend is
continuous improvement — as happened with the internet, once impractical and now
basic infrastructure. Buckle up and know thyself: knowing which group you are in
is the best path to handling this transition and preserving your sanity.
